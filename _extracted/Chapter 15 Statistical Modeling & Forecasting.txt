15.1 What Is a Statistical Model?
A statistical model is a mathematical representation of relationships observed in data.
Rather than attempting to recreate reality perfectly, a model simplifies reality while retaining the characteristics that are relevant to the research question.
Examples:
Relationship between volatility and trading volume.
Relationship between interest rates and gold prices.
Relationship between market regime and strategy performance.
Every model is an approximation—not a perfect description of the market.
15.2 Why Models Matter
Models help researchers:
Describe relationships.
Explain historical behavior.
Estimate unknown values.
Forecast future observations.
Quantify uncertainty.
Test hypotheses.
Within QROS, models support understanding before prediction.

15.4 Descriptive vs Predictive Models
Not all models aim to forecast.
Descriptive Models
Purpose:
Explain relationships.
Example:
"How does volatility change during London trading hours?"
Predictive Models
Purpose:
Estimate future values.
Example:
"What is the probability that gold will move more than 1% tomorrow?"
Both approaches are valuable and often complement one another.
15.5 Linear Regression
Linear regression is one of the most widely used statistical tools.
It estimates how one variable changes in relation to another.
Example:
Gold Return = f(U.S. Dollar Index Return)
Possible uses:
Measuring sensitivity.
Estimating relationships.
Feature screening.
Baseline forecasting.
Although financial markets are often non-linear, linear regression remains an important benchmark.
15.6 Multiple Regression
Many market phenomena depend on several variables simultaneously.
Example:
Gold Return =
Dollar Index
Treasury Yield
Oil Price
Volatility
Inflation Expectations
Multiple regression enables researchers to estimate the combined influence of several explanatory variables while controlling for the effects of others.
15.7 Model Assumptions
Statistical models rely on assumptions.
Common assumptions include:
Independent observations.
Constant variance.
Normally distributed residuals.
Linear relationships.
No severe multicollinearity.
Financial data frequently violates these assumptions.
Rather than ignoring this reality, QROS requires researchers to assess and document assumption validity.
15.8 Residuals
Residuals are the differences between observed values and model predictions.
Residual analysis helps answer questions such as:
Is the model systematically biased?
Does unexplained variation remain?
Are important variables missing?
A well-performing model should leave residuals that behave approximately like random noise.
15.9 Forecasting Models
Forecasting extends descriptive models into future estimation.
Common statistical forecasting families include:
Autoregressive (AR)
Future values depend on previous values.
Moving Average (MA)
Future estimates incorporate previous forecast errors.
ARMA
Combines autoregressive and moving-average components.
ARIMA
Extends ARMA by incorporating differencing to address non-stationary series.
ARIMA remains a foundational forecasting approach in quantitative finance despite the growth of machine learning.
15.10 Model Evaluation
Forecast quality should be evaluated objectively.
Common measures include:
Mean Absolute Error (MAE).
Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE).
Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE).
R² (Coefficient of Determination).
Directional Accuracy.
Different metrics emphasize different aspects of performance.
No single metric is sufficient in isolation.
15.11 Overfitting
A model that perfectly explains historical data may fail on new observations.
This phenomenon is known as overfitting.
Symptoms include:
Exceptional in-sample performance.
Poor out-of-sample performance.
Excessive parameter complexity.
QROS prioritizes generalization over historical perfection.
15.12 Model Robustness
A useful model should remain effective under changing conditions.
Robustness testing includes:
Different market regimes.
Alternative sampling periods.
Varying parameter values.
Transaction costs.
Structural breaks.
The objective is to identify models that remain useful rather than those optimized for a single historical period.
15.13 Forecast Uncertainty
Forecasts are estimates, not certainties.
Instead of reporting:
"Tomorrow's price will be 3,450."
Researchers should communicate uncertainty.
Example:
"There is a 70% probability that tomorrow's closing price will fall between 3,430 and 3,470."
Expressing uncertainty aligns with the probabilistic philosophy established earlier in the handbook.

QROS Engineering Specification
Model Acceptance Standard (MAS-1)
Before deployment, a statistical model must:
Address a clearly defined research question.
Document assumptions.
Demonstrate reproducible results.
Pass out-of-sample validation.
Include diagnostic analysis.
Quantify forecast uncertainty.
Undergo peer review.
Receive formal approval.
Models that fail these criteria remain experimental.
Case Study
A researcher develops a regression model suggesting that gold prices rise when the U.S. Dollar Index declines.
Before accepting the model, the researcher:
Tests the relationship across multiple market regimes.
Examines residual behavior.
Evaluates predictive accuracy on unseen data.
Investigates whether the relationship changes during major macroeconomic events.
Documents assumptions and limitations.
Only after completing these steps does the model become eligible for integration into the broader research pipeline.
Chapter Summary
Statistical models provide a structured framework for describing, explaining, and forecasting financial markets. By emphasizing transparency, diagnostics, validation, and governance, QROS ensures that models contribute reliable evidence rather than merely fitting historical data.
These methods form the bridge between exploratory research and advanced machine learning, providing interpretable baselines against which more complex approaches can be evaluated.
