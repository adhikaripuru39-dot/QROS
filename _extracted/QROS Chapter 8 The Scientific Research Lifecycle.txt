8.1 Why Research Needs a Process
Imagine two researchers studying the same market.
Researcher A
He opens TradingView.
He notices that gold bounced from a moving average three times.
He immediately builds an Expert Advisor.
He optimizes parameters.
The backtest looks fantastic.
He starts live trading.
Three weeks later:
The strategy fails.
He starts again.
Researcher B
He notices the same observation.
Instead of coding immediately he asks:
Why did price react?
Was it coincidence?
Does this happen in all market regimes?
Is the sample statistically meaningful?
Does the effect remain after trading costs?
What alternative explanations exist?
Only after answering these questions does he begin coding.
The second researcher is following a scientific process.
QROS is built for Researcher B.
8.2 The Philosophy of Research
Research is not the process of proving yourself correct.
Research is the process of discovering whether your idea survives objective testing.
One of the greatest dangers in trading is becoming emotionally attached to a strategy.
QROS therefore adopts the following principle:
The goal of research is not to confirm ideas. The goal is to eliminate weak ideas and strengthen robust ones.
This mindset reduces confirmation bias and encourages intellectual honesty.

8.4 Stage 1 – Market Observation
Every project begins with curiosity.
Observation examples:
Gold tends to trend after major CPI surprises.
London session has higher volatility.
Pullbacks during strong trends appear shorter.
Volatility increases before FOMC announcements.
An observation is not a conclusion.
Its purpose is to identify something worth investigating.
At this stage:
No trading rules are written.
No optimization is performed.
No assumptions are accepted as facts.
The objective is simply to identify a potentially meaningful market behavior.
8.5 Stage 2 – Problem Definition
A vague observation becomes a clearly defined problem.
Weak statement:
"Gold moves strangely during news."
Strong statement:
"The impact of high-impact U.S. economic announcements on the short-term volatility of XAUUSD is not well quantified within the current research framework."
Good problem statements are:
Specific.
Measurable.
Relevant.
Narrow enough to investigate.
8.6 Stage 3 – Research Question
The research question guides the entire project.
Examples:
Does realized volatility increase significantly during the first 15 minutes after CPI releases?
Does the trend continuation probability depend on the prior volatility regime?
Does spread widening reduce expected value during news trading?
A good research question should be answerable through evidence.
8.7 Stage 4 – Hypothesis Formulation
Every research question leads to one or more hypotheses.
Example:
Null Hypothesis (H₀):
CPI announcements do not significantly affect XAUUSD volatility.
Alternative Hypothesis (H₁):
CPI announcements significantly increase XAUUSD volatility during the first 15 minutes after release.
A hypothesis must be:
Testable.
Falsifiable.
Measurable.
Time-bound.
Independent of personal belief.
8.8 Stage 5 – Research Planning
Before collecting data, QROS requires a documented research plan.
The plan includes:
Objective
What is the project trying to discover?
Scope
Which market?
Which timeframe?
Which historical period?
Required Data
Price data.
Volume.
Economic calendar.
Spreads.
Order execution statistics.
Statistical Methods
Regression.
Correlation.
Hypothesis testing.
Monte Carlo.
Walk-forward analysis.
Success Criteria
How will we decide whether the hypothesis is supported?
Planning before analysis reduces the temptation to change objectives after seeing the results.
8.9 Stage 6 – Data Collection
Data quality determines research quality.
QROS requires every dataset to document:
Source.
Collection date.
Time zone.
Sampling frequency.
Missing values.
Licensing restrictions.
Version.
Data should be treated as a managed asset, not just a file.
8.10 Stage 7 – Data Validation
Before analysis begins:
Check for missing observations.
Verify timestamps.
Detect duplicates.
Confirm trading sessions.
Identify suspicious values.
Compare against independent sources where practical.
No project proceeds until the data passes validation.
8.11 Stage 8 – Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)
EDA helps researchers understand the data before building models.
Typical activities include:
Plotting price distributions.
Examining volatility over time.
Calculating descriptive statistics.
Detecting seasonality.
Identifying outliers.
Measuring correlations.
EDA often reveals issues or opportunities that were not apparent during planning.
8.12 Stage 9 – Feature Engineering
Raw market data is transformed into informative variables.
Examples:
ATR.
Realized volatility.
Session indicators.
Rolling correlations.
Momentum measures.
Liquidity metrics.
Feature engineering bridges raw data and predictive modeling.
8.13 Stage 10 – Model Development
Only now is it appropriate to develop a strategy or predictive model.
Potential approaches include:
Rule-based systems.
Statistical models.
Machine learning algorithms.
Hybrid frameworks.
The chosen approach should align with the original research question rather than the availability of a particular algorithm.
8.14 Stage 11 – Validation
Validation extends beyond a single backtest.
QROS requires multiple layers of evidence:
In-sample testing.
Out-of-sample testing.
Walk-forward validation.
Sensitivity analysis.
Monte Carlo simulation.
Stress testing.
Transaction cost modeling.
Each layer addresses a different source of uncertainty.
8.15 Stage 12 – Governance Review
Before production, the research undergoes structured review.
Key questions include:
Does the evidence support the hypothesis?
Were the methods appropriate?
Is the documentation complete?
Have risks been identified?
Can the results be reproduced?
Approval is based on evidence, not enthusiasm.
8.16 Stage 13 – Deployment
Deployment begins cautiously.
Typical progression:

Scaling is earned through consistent performance under real conditions.
8.17 Stage 14 – Continuous Monitoring
Research does not end when a strategy goes live.
The platform continuously monitors:
Performance drift.
Regime changes.
Execution quality.
Risk metrics.
Data quality.
Model stability.
When evidence indicates deterioration, the strategy returns to the research pipeline.
8.18 Institutional Memory
Every completed project—successful or unsuccessful—adds to the QROS knowledge base.
Required artifacts include:
Research proposal.
Data dictionary.
Experiment log.
Statistical report.
Validation report.
Decision record.
Deployment report.
Lessons learned.
This institutional memory prevents repeated mistakes and accelerates future research.

Case Study
A researcher believes:
"Gold tends to reverse after large one-minute candles."
Instead of immediately coding a reversal strategy, the researcher:
Defines what qualifies as a "large" candle.
Collects five years of XAUUSD data.
Measures reversal probability under different volatility regimes.
Tests whether the pattern remains after spreads and slippage.
Validates the findings on unseen data.
Documents all assumptions and limitations.
Whether the hypothesis is confirmed or rejected, the result becomes valuable institutional knowledge because the process was rigorous and reproducible.
Chapter Summary
Professional quantitative research is a structured process, not a sequence of isolated experiments. QROS transforms observations into knowledge through disciplined planning, high-quality data, rigorous validation, and documented governance.
By standardizing the research lifecycle, the platform ensures that every strategy is evaluated consistently and that knowledge accumulates over time rather than being lost between projects.
